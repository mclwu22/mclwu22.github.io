Generative Sequential Recommendation on Amazon Reviews 2023

Course project · ECE 8803 (ST: Conversational AI), Georgia Tech · Fall 2025

An end-to-end generative sequential recommendation pipeline built in two connected halves: a PySpark stage that turns 59.77M raw Amazon reviews into a clean leave-one-out benchmark, and a reproduction of TIGER — RQ-VAE Semantic IDs feeding generative retrieval. Everything runs on one machine (32 cores, 251 GB RAM, RTX 6000 Ada): no cluster, no cloud. The Spark half retraces a pipeline demonstrated in the course, rebuilt and instrumented from scratch; the TIGER half is my own implementation. Either way the point was less to hit a number than to measure where the time actually goes, and to report what the measurements said even when they said "no effect."

PySpark RQ-VAE Semantic IDs Generative retrieval Sentence-T5 Single-node

Design judgment

A 40 GB working set against 251 GB of RAM. A cluster would only have added shuffle overhead across the network for data that already fits in memory, so the pipeline runs in Spark local mode and the effort goes into the bottlenecks that actually bind at this scale: caching in iterative jobs, join strategy, and partition-level skew. Distribution is a cost, not a default.

Caching turned a killed job into 237 seconds

k-core filtering is iterative, and Spark's lazy DAG recomputes the whole lineage on every round unless you break it. Uncached, round 6 alone took 188 s — 15× the cached round — and round 7 exceeded 250 s and was killed. Adding .cache() converged all 8 rounds in 237 s.

Skew is a property of the partition key, not of the data

Item frequency is sharply power-law: the hottest item has 13,970 interactions, about 1160× the median of 12. The intuitive conclusion is that stragglers will dominate. Measured at 256 partitions, row-level imbalance was only 1.05× by user and 1.18× by item — because no single key is large enough to overflow a partition. The skew that matters is the skew relative to partition size.

Broadcast joins have a size cliff, and you can move it

Broadcasting the full 3.5M-row metadata table failed outright: the serialized payload exceeded 1 GB and blew past the driver's maxResultSize. Filtering down to the 715k relevant items first made it broadcastable and 1.37× faster than the SortMergeJoin. I confirmed with .explain() that the physical plan actually flipped from SortMergeJoin to BroadcastHashJoin, rather than assuming the hint took.

Adaptive Query Execution: no effect (a negative result worth reporting)

AQE on vs. off measured 7.0 s vs 8.2 s — within noise and in the wrong direction to claim a win. AQE exists to fix skewed and misestimated shuffles; this data was already balanced, so there was nothing for it to repair. Reporting it as a null result is more useful than quietly dropping the experiment.

Instrumentation as a deliverable

Shipped as a parameterized pipeline with a README, a BENCHMARKS file recording every timing above, an environment audit so the numbers are reproducible on a known machine, and a 10-lesson Spark onboarding notebook.

Part B — TIGER reproduction

Stage 1 — trained

Semantic IDs from an RQ-VAE over frozen text embeddings

Sentence-T5-base (frozen) · residual vector quantization

Each item's text is encoded once by a frozen Sentence-T5-base, giving 715,729 × 768 embeddings. An RQ-VAE compresses 768 → 32 and quantizes the residual in three stages against 3 codebooks × 256 entries, trained with a straight-through estimator, k-means codebook initialization, and dead-code revival.

Training100 epochs / ~200 s
Reconstruction loss0.67 → 0.42
Codebook utilization100% at all three levels — no collapse
Unique 3-code combinations484k
Collisions231.6k, resolved with a 4th token
Outputevery item → Semantic ID (c1, c2, c3, c_collision)

Codebook collapse is the standard failure mode here — a few codes absorb everything and the rest go dead, silently destroying the ID space. k-means initialization plus dead-code revival held utilization at 100% across all three levels, which is the result that makes the ID space usable downstream.

Stage 2 — architecture and evaluation protocol

Generative retrieval over the Semantic ID space

T5 encoder-decoder · constrained beam search

Stage 2 turns retrieval into sequence generation. Each user's interaction history is rewritten as a sequence of semantic tokens, and a T5 encoder-decoder is trained to generate the Semantic ID of the next item directly — replacing an embedding lookup over a 715k-item catalog with a decode over a four-token vocabulary. Decoding is constrained so that beam search can only emit valid, in-catalog ID prefixes, and the model is scored with Recall@K and NDCG@K on the leave-one-out split built in Part A.

Training results for Stage 2 are not reported here.

What I took away

Spark internals

  • Lazy evaluation: transformations vs. actions, and why iterative jobs recompute
  • Narrow vs. wide dependencies; shuffle and partition tuning
  • Caching as the fix for repeated lineage in iterative algorithms
  • Broadcast vs. sort-merge join, and the driver-side size cliff
  • Reading physical plans with .explain() instead of trusting hints
  • Quantifying skew against partition size; AQE and when it does nothing
  • Parquet, Spark UI diagnostics

Modeling

  • Vector and residual quantization; RQ-VAE
  • Straight-through estimator for non-differentiable quantization
  • Codebook collapse and its mitigations (k-means init, dead-code revival)
  • Semantic IDs as a discrete item representation
  • Generative retrieval (TIGER) for sequential recommendation
  • Leave-one-out evaluation protocol

Engineering judgment

  • Choosing local mode from the data-to-memory ratio rather than by habit
  • Treating instrumentation and benchmarks as deliverables
  • Publishing negative results instead of burying them

Next steps

  1. Train the Stage 2 T5 encoder-decoder on the semantic token sequences and report Recall@K / NDCG@K against sequential baselines on the Part A split.
  2. Ablate the collision token: measure how much of the retrieval quality depends on the 4th token that disambiguates the 231.6k colliding items.
  3. Test whether Semantic ID structure helps in the cold-start regime, where a generative decoder should degrade more gracefully than an ID-embedding lookup.
  4. Capture Spark UI execution traces to back the Part A timings with stage-level evidence rather than wall-clock alone.
