Research

Deep learning in medical imaging is usually built one task at a time: a model for tumor segmentation, another for disease classification, another for outcome prediction — each needing its own carefully annotated dataset. Labels are the bottleneck, and in clinical imaging they are expensive, inconsistent, and scarce.

My work asks whether a single representation, learned without labels, can serve all of those tasks instead. That question splits into the three threads I spend most of my time on: how to pretrain at scale on messy real-world image collections, how to evaluate a representation honestly rather than by tuning it into a good score, and how far a representation carries across the shifts that break models in practice — different scanners, different institutions, different modalities.

More recently I have been reading into LLM-based agents for clinical workflows, where the interesting problem is grounding an agent's decisions in a domain-pretrained perception model rather than in prompting alone.

Selected projects

BrainDINO — one brain MRI representation across many clinical tasks

First author
Self-supervised foundation model · DINO-style self-distillation · arXiv:2604.27277 · code
6.6Munlabeled slices pretrained on
20public neuroimaging datasets
7downstream task families
0labels used in pretraining

A slice-wise brain MRI foundation model trained with a DINO-style teacher–student objective on approximately 6.6 million unlabeled axial slices (52,000+ 3D volumes) drawn from 20 heterogeneous public datasets, spanning wide variation in population, disease, scanner, and acquisition protocol.

  • Strict subject-level separation between pretraining and evaluation, so that transfer results measure generalization rather than memorization under cross-dataset distribution shift.
  • Evaluation keeps the backbone frozen and trains only lightweight task heads — isolating representation quality from fine-tuning capacity, and making comparisons across tasks fair.
  • Tested across tumor segmentation, disease classification (autism spectrum disorder, mild cognitive impairment), brain-age and post-stroke temporal estimation, survival prediction, MRI sequence classification, and molecular mutation prediction.
  • Matches or exceeds natural-image pretraining and MRI-specific self-supervised baselines across 10–100% label regimes, with the widest margins where supervision is scarcest.
  • Representation analysis (CKA, embedding visualization) shows anatomically organized and pathology-sensitive structure emerging without any task supervision.

MedDINOv3 — adapting vision foundation models to medical segmentation

Co-author
Domain-adaptive self-supervised pretraining · arXiv:2509.02379 · Jun – Oct 2025

How much of a natural-image foundation model survives the jump to medical imaging, and what has to be rebuilt? We adapted the DINOv3 self-distillation framework to dense visual representation learning under large appearance and distribution shift, refining a ViT backbone with multi-scale token aggregation for segmentation.

  • Pretrained on CT-3M — 3.8M+ slices assembled from 16 datasets.
  • Evaluated cross-modality and cross-domain transfer between CT and MRI benchmarks.

3D-MedDCNet — detecting small brain metastases

First author
3D deformable convolutional network · Medical Physics (2025) · Dec 2024 – May 2025

Brain metastases are small, numerous, and unevenly distributed — a hard case for detection networks tuned on large, compact lesions. I developed a 3D deformable convolutional architecture for detecting and segmenting them on MRI, improving robustness to extreme scale variation and sparse lesion distribution.

AnatoMask — reconstruction-guided self-supervised segmentation

Co-author
Self-masking strategy · ECCV 2024 · 2023 – 2024

Rather than masking image patches at random, AnatoMask uses reconstruction difficulty to decide what to hide — steering the pretext task toward anatomically meaningful regions and improving robustness and generalization under limited-label supervision.
